When the Sermon Becomes Bigger Than Scripture

November 22, 2026  •  7 min read

There is a difference between preaching the Word of God and using the Word of God to preach a message. That distinction may seem small, but it is one the Church cannot afford to ignore.

A preacher can stand before thousands, speak passionately about Jesus, quote Scripture, and leave an entire congregation feeling encouraged. The music may be moving, the message may be powerful, and the people may walk away believing they have heard something profound. But none of those things establish whether the Word of God was faithfully taught.

The question is not whether the sermon made people feel something. The question is whether the preacher accurately communicated what God has said.

“Preach the word; be ready in season and out of season; reprove, rebuke, exhort, with great patience and teaching.” — 2 Timothy 4:2 (LSB)

Paul did not instruct Timothy to entertain the congregation, manufacture excitement, or reshape Scripture into something more appealing. He commanded him to preach the Word. That means the message must come from Scripture, rather than Scripture being made to serve a message the preacher has already decided to deliver.

This is where the Church must exercise discernment, particularly when examining influential pastors such as Steven Furtick of Elevation Church.

Furtick is an engaging speaker. His sermons are energetic, emotionally compelling, and frequently centered on encouragement, personal struggles, and overcoming adversity. None of those qualities are inherently wrong. Scripture itself contains encouragement, comfort, and hope. But a pastor’s responsibility extends far beyond making people feel encouraged.

A motivational speaker can inspire people to overcome difficult circumstances. A shepherd is called to teach the whole counsel of God, including the parts that confront our sin, challenge our desires, and call us to repentance.

Those responsibilities are not interchangeable.

One concern worth examining is how Scripture is presented and interpreted. Furtick has preached from the NIV and has used other English translations in his teaching. Using multiple translations is not inherently improper, nor does a word-for-word translation automatically prevent someone from mishandling Scripture. Nevertheless, there is an important distinction between translations that attempt to preserve the wording and structure of the original languages and those that prioritize communicating their meaning in contemporary language.

Translations such as the Legacy Standard Bible and New American Standard Bible generally follow a more formally equivalent approach. Others, such as the New Living Translation, place greater emphasis on conveying meaning through natural English expression.

A pastor should understand these differences. More importantly, he should be able to demonstrate that his interpretation comes from the meaning of the biblical text, rather than from whichever English rendering best supports his sermon.

The congregation deserves more than a collection of verses arranged around an appealing idea. They deserve to understand what those passages mean, why they were written, and how they fit within the larger testimony of Scripture.

Consider one particularly troubling statement attributed to Furtick’s preaching: that God “broke the law for love.”

The statement was presented through an illustration involving a parent who might break a traffic law to reach a child in an emergency. The intended emphasis appears to be the extraordinary nature of God’s love and His willingness to rescue sinners.

That context matters. It would be unfair to isolate the statement and immediately conclude that Furtick intended to teach that Jesus committed sin. But understanding the illustration does not eliminate the theological problem it introduces.

God is not a desperate parent forced to choose between obeying a law and saving someone He loves. He is the Creator of heaven and earth, the giver of the Law, and the very standard of righteousness.

God’s love does not require Him to abandon His justice.

“For what the Law could not do, weak as it was through the flesh, God did: sending His own Son in the likeness of sinful flesh and as an offering for sin, He condemned sin in the flesh.” — Romans 8:3 (LSB)

Paul does not describe God setting aside His righteousness to save humanity. He describes God accomplishing what fallen humanity could not accomplish through the Law. The weakness was not a moral defect in God’s Law, but the sinfulness of human flesh.

Christ did not come to violate God’s righteousness. He came to fulfill the Law.

Jesus Himself declared in Matthew 5:17 that He did not come to abolish the Law or the Prophets, but to fulfill them.

And in Romans 3:26, Paul explains that God demonstrated His righteousness so that He would be both just and the justifier of the one who has faith in Jesus.

That is the beauty of the gospel. God did not cease to be righteous in order to show mercy. His mercy was accomplished without compromising His righteousness.

There is also a distinction between God’s unchanging moral character and the particular covenant commands given to Israel. Jesus challenged human traditions and interpretations that had been imposed upon God’s Law. His confrontations with the religious leaders do not establish that He sinned against the Law itself.

This matters because illustrations are not harmless simply because they sound powerful. When an illustration suggests something contrary to the character of God, the preacher has a responsibility to clarify it. Otherwise, the congregation may remember the illustration more clearly than the Scripture it was supposed to explain.

Another example concerns Furtick’s discussion of God’s love being perfected among believers. In a message drawing from 1 John 4, he used the image of the cross to illustrate God’s love toward humanity and the love Christians are called to show one another.

The underlying biblical principle is sound: those who have received God’s love are commanded to love others.

But describing the cross as somehow incomplete until believers love one another risks confusing two very different truths.

“No one has beheld God at any time; if we love one another, God abides in us, and His love is perfected in us.” — 1 John 4:12 (LSB)

John is speaking about God’s love reaching its intended expression in the lives of believers. He is not teaching that Christ’s sacrifice remains unfinished until Christians demonstrate love.

Jesus declared, “It is finished” in John 19:30.

Our obedience is a response to the finished work of Christ, not something that completes His atonement.

Even when a preacher’s intended message is legitimate, the language used to communicate it matters. A congregation should not have to untangle a theological contradiction to discover the biblical truth hidden underneath an illustration.

This is not an argument that every poorly worded statement makes someone a false teacher. Every pastor is capable of speaking imprecisely, and every sermon should be evaluated fairly, with attention to context. But repeated carelessness in handling Scripture deserves serious scrutiny, especially when the teacher has influence over thousands of people.

“Let not many of you become teachers, my brothers, knowing that we will receive a stricter judgment.” — James 3:1 (LSB)

James reminds us that teaching God’s Word carries extraordinary responsibility. The greater the influence, the greater the need for care, humility, and accountability.

The problem is not limited to Steven Furtick, Elevation Church, or any particular denomination. It is a danger present wherever personality becomes more important than doctrine, emotional response becomes the measure of spiritual growth, and Scripture is treated as material from which to construct an appealing presentation.

Paul warned Timothy that a time would come when people would not endure sound doctrine, but would gather teachers who satisfied what they wanted to hear (2 Timothy 4:3–4).

That warning should concern both the preacher and the congregation.

We must be willing to hear the parts of Scripture that make us uncomfortable. We must be willing to confront sin rather than simply seek encouragement. And we must be willing to examine even the teachers we admire.

The Bereans were commended in Acts 17:11 because they examined the Scriptures daily to determine whether the things they were being taught were true. They did this while listening to the apostle Paul. If Paul’s teaching was examined against Scripture, no modern pastor should expect exemption from that same scrutiny.

This does not mean approaching every sermon with hostility or assuming the worst about every preacher. It means remembering that the authority belongs to God’s Word, not to the person standing behind the pulpit.

A faithful shepherd does not need to make Scripture more exciting by changing its meaning. The Word of God is already living, powerful, and sufficient.

The Church does not need another motivational speaker dressed as a pastor. It needs men who will faithfully teach the Scriptures, explain them in context, proclaim Christ, confront sin, and shepherd the people entrusted to their care.

Because when the sermon becomes more important than the Scripture, the congregation may leave feeling inspired while remaining ignorant of what God has actually said.

And that should trouble every Christian who claims to love His Word.
