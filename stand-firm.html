Stand Firm

October 25, 2026  •  13 min read

There’s something I’ve been thinking about lately when it comes to spiritual warfare, and I think it’s something Christians need to take a little more seriously.

The armor of God.

Most of us have probably heard about it. Maybe you learned about the different pieces in Sunday school, heard a pastor preach through Ephesians 6, or even memorized the passage when you were younger.

But here’s my question.

How many of us actually understand what it means to put on the full armor of God?

And more importantly, how many of us are actually living that way?

In Ephesians 6:10–20, Paul addresses spiritual warfare and explains how Christians are supposed to stand against the schemes of the devil. He describes six pieces of armor, each representing something essential to our spiritual lives.

But I think there’s a danger in how familiar we’ve become with this passage.

We know the names of the armor. We know we’re supposed to put it on. We might even pray about it in the morning before heading off to work.

But knowing about the armor and actually standing firm in Christ are two very different things.

Paul begins in Ephesians 6:10 (LSB) by telling us to “be strong in the Lord and in the might of His strength.”

Notice something about that.

He doesn’t tell us to be strong in ourselves.

Not in our knowledge. Not in our abilities. Not in how long we’ve been Christians or how many Bible verses we’ve memorized.

Our strength comes from the Lord.

And that matters because we’re talking about an enemy who has been deceiving mankind since the Garden of Eden.

Think about Adam and Eve for a moment.

Satan didn’t need to physically overpower them. He questioned what God had said, distorted the truth, and convinced Eve to doubt God’s command.

And what happened?

Sin entered the world.

Now look at how Satan tempted Jesus in Matthew 4. He even used Scripture, but he twisted its application to serve his purpose.

Jesus responded with the truth of God’s Word.

There’s something we need to understand here.

Satan doesn’t always need some dramatic supernatural encounter to influence someone’s life. Sometimes deception begins with something as simple as questioning what God has already made clear.

A little compromise.

A little doubt.

A little justification for something we know we shouldn’t be doing.

Then another.

And another.

Eventually, we can find ourselves defending the very things Scripture warns us against.

And that’s where the armor of God becomes so important.

Something I find particularly interesting is that the armor Paul describes wasn’t first introduced in Ephesians.

Look at Isaiah 59:17.

Hundreds of years before Paul’s letter, Isaiah described God putting on righteousness as a breastplate and salvation as a helmet. Isaiah 11:5 also connects righteousness and faithfulness with a belt, while Isaiah 52:7 speaks of the beautiful feet of the one bringing good news and announcing peace.

Sound familiar?

Paul wasn’t simply looking at a Roman soldier and coming up with a clever illustration.

He was drawing upon imagery already found in Scripture, imagery associated with God Himself.

Think about the significance of that.

The armor we’re commanded to put on isn’t something we’re expected to manufacture through our own goodness or spiritual accomplishments.

God provides it.

We’re called to take it up.

And there’s an important distinction between those two things.

We’re not fighting spiritual battles by trying to become powerful enough to defeat Satan ourselves. We’re standing upon what God has provided through His truth, righteousness, salvation, and Word.

But we also need to understand what we’re standing against.

Ephesians 6:12 reminds us that our struggle isn’t ultimately against flesh and blood. Paul identifies spiritual forces of wickedness as the opposition.

Now, before someone takes that to mean every person who disagrees with them is being controlled by a demon, that’s not what I’m saying.

We need discernment.

Not every bad day is a demonic attack. Not every disagreement is spiritual warfare. Sometimes we face the consequences of our own foolish decisions, and sometimes we’re simply dealing with the difficulties of living in a fallen world.

But pretending Satan isn’t actively working against the things of God would be equally foolish.

First Peter 5:8 warns Christians to remain sober-minded and watchful because the devil is actively seeking someone to devour.

That should get our attention.

Here’s something else worth considering.

Ephesus wasn’t a city unfamiliar with spiritual practices. Acts 19:18–20 describes people who had practiced magic turning away from those things, with many publicly burning their books.

These were people who had previously looked to practices associated with supernatural power.

Yet Paul doesn’t instruct the Ephesian believers to protect themselves through rituals, special objects, or mystical formulas.

He tells them to put on the armor of God.

Do you see the difference?

It’s not about finding some spiritual shortcut to protection.

It’s about depending upon the Lord and living according to His Word.

That brings me to the actual pieces of armor.

Paul begins with truth and righteousness.

Think about how those two things work together.

How can we recognize deception if we don’t know the truth? And what good is claiming to know God’s truth if we continually refuse to live according to it?

Jesus said in John 17:17 that God’s Word is truth.

We cannot expect to recognize the enemy’s lies if we’re spending more time listening to the world than studying what God has actually said.

And righteousness matters just as much.

Our righteousness before God comes through Jesus Christ, not through our ability to earn salvation. Second Corinthians 5:21 makes that clear.

But being saved by grace doesn’t mean we should become comfortable living in sin.

We’re called to grow in holiness.

I’m not saying Christians are supposed to be perfect. I’m certainly not, and neither is anyone else walking this earth.

We all struggle.

But there’s a difference between struggling against sin and continually making excuses to remain in it.

You cannot knowingly embrace the very things God calls you to turn away from while acting as though spiritual readiness has nothing to do with obedience.

Paul then speaks about our feet being prepared with the gospel of peace.

And I find that interesting.

We’re discussing spiritual warfare, yet part of our armor is associated with peace.

Not the kind of peace the world offers by pretending everything is fine, but the peace that comes through reconciliation with God through Jesus Christ.

Romans 5:1 explains that believers have peace with God through our Lord Jesus Christ.

That is the foundation we stand upon.

But the gospel isn’t something we’re supposed to keep to ourselves, either.

We’re called to be prepared to share the hope we have in Christ.

And I’ll come back to that in a moment because I think it’s especially important when we get to the end of Paul’s passage.

Next is the shield of faith.

Paul describes it as protection against the flaming arrows of the evil one.

Have you ever noticed how easily fear, doubt, or discouragement can begin influencing your decisions?

Maybe you know what Scripture says about something, but following it might cost you a relationship, an opportunity, or the approval of people around you.

Suddenly, trusting God doesn’t seem quite so easy.

You begin considering whether compromise would really be that bad.

Maybe just this once.

But faith isn’t about trusting God only when obedience is convenient.

It’s trusting Him when circumstances are uncertain, when the world disagrees, and when following Christ costs us something.

That doesn’t mean we never experience fear or doubt.

It means we continue placing our trust in Him rather than allowing those things to determine how we live.

Then Paul tells us to receive the helmet of salvation and the sword of the Spirit, which he identifies as the Word of God.

Our salvation is found in Jesus Christ. That truth should shape how we think, how we respond to temptation, and how we view our circumstances.

And the sword?

God’s Word.

Not our opinions. Not whatever happens to be culturally acceptable. Not some motivational phrase that makes us feel better about ourselves.

Scripture.

Hebrews 4:12 describes the Word of God as living, active, and sharper than any two-edged sword.

And this is why I believe Christians need to spend time actually studying their Bibles.

Not just reading a verse on social media.

Not just listening to someone explain what Scripture supposedly says.

Open your Bible. Read the passage. Understand the context. Compare Scripture with Scripture.

We should absolutely learn from pastors and teachers, but we also have a responsibility to examine what we’re being taught.

Because if someone twists Scripture, how are we going to recognize it if we don’t know what Scripture says?

Now, there’s something else about this armor that I think we sometimes overlook.

Roman soldiers didn’t always fight as isolated individuals. They could fight together, using coordinated formations and their shields to protect one another.

Paul doesn’t explicitly tell Christians to form a Roman shield formation, so I don’t want to force that illustration into the passage.

But it does make me think about something.

Why do we sometimes act as though we’re supposed to face every spiritual struggle alone?

We have brothers and sisters in Christ for a reason.

Hebrews 10:24–25 reminds believers of the importance of gathering together and encouraging one another.

We need godly fellowship.

We need people who will encourage us when we’re struggling, pray for us when we’re discouraged, and correct us when we’re heading in the wrong direction.

And sometimes that correction isn’t going to be comfortable.

But I’d rather have someone who loves me enough to tell me the truth than surround myself with people who will watch me drift away from the Lord without saying a word.

Spiritual isolation is dangerous.

And that’s one reason the next part of Paul’s instruction is so important.

Most discussions about the armor of God spend considerable time on verses 10–17.

But Paul doesn’t stop there.

In verse 18, he continues by instructing believers to pray at all times in the Spirit, remaining alert and persevering in prayer for all the saints.

Think about that.

After explaining everything we need to stand firm, Paul immediately directs our attention to prayer.

Prayer isn’t explicitly called a seventh piece of armor, but it’s certainly not an optional afterthought.

We’re supposed to be depending upon God continually.

Yet how often do we do the opposite?

We face a problem, try to figure everything out ourselves, become frustrated when things don’t work, and only then turn to prayer.

I’ve certainly been guilty of that.

We say we trust God, but sometimes our actions suggest we’re only willing to surrender control after we’ve exhausted every other option.

That’s something worth examining.

And notice that Paul doesn’t tell us to pray only for ourselves.

He tells us to pray for all the saints.

We’re supposed to be supporting one another spiritually, not simply worrying about our own circumstances.

But here’s where I think this passage becomes especially challenging.

Verses 19 and 20.

Paul asks believers to pray for him so that he would be given the words to proclaim the mystery of the gospel boldly.

Now remember something.

Paul was imprisoned.

He describes himself as an ambassador in chains.

And what does he ask people to pray for?

Boldness to proclaim Christ.

Think about that for a moment.

Here’s a man who has suffered tremendously for the gospel, who is literally in chains, and he’s asking other Christians to pray that he will continue speaking boldly.

How many of us would have made that our prayer request?

Would we have asked for courage to proclaim Christ?

Or would our only concern have been getting out of the circumstances we were facing?

I’m not saying there’s anything wrong with praying for deliverance. Scripture gives us plenty of examples of people crying out to God for help.

But Paul understood that his circumstances didn’t change his calling.

And neither do ours.

We are still called to share the gospel.

We’re still called to stand upon biblical truth.

We’re still called to live in a way that reflects Jesus Christ.

Even when it’s uncomfortable.

Even when people disagree.

Even when doing so costs us something.

I’ve been thinking quite a bit lately about our responsibility to share Christ with others.

If we knew someone was about to do something that could seriously harm them, most of us wouldn’t hesitate to warn them.

We’d speak up because we cared.

Yet when it comes to someone’s eternal destination, how often do we remain silent because we’re worried about offending them?

Now, I’m not suggesting we should chase people around, argue them into submission, or treat every conversation as an opportunity to win a theological debate.

That’s not how we demonstrate the love of Christ.

But genuine love shouldn’t be confused with remaining silent about the truth.

We cannot save anyone.

Only God can change a heart.

First Corinthians 3:6 reminds us that Paul planted, Apollos watered, but God was responsible for the growth.

Our responsibility is to be faithful.

Maybe something we share plants a seed that takes years to grow.

Maybe it’s a conversation someone remembers long after we’ve forgotten about it.

We might never know how God uses those moments.

But that shouldn’t stop us from being willing to speak.

And that’s what makes Paul’s request in Ephesians 6:19–20 so significant to me.

He wasn’t simply asking God to help him withstand spiritual opposition.

He wanted boldness to continue proclaiming the very gospel for which he was suffering.

So when we talk about putting on the armor of God, I think we need to consider the entire passage.

Not just the six pieces.

Not just resisting temptation.

Not just protecting ourselves from the schemes of the devil.

We’re called to stand firm in the Lord, remain grounded in His Word, persevere in prayer, support one another, and faithfully proclaim Jesus Christ.

And I want to leave you with a few questions.

How much time are you actually spending in God’s Word?

Are you allowing Scripture to shape your decisions, or are you finding ways to justify decisions you’ve already made?

When life becomes difficult, is prayer your first response or your last resort?

Are you surrounding yourself with believers who encourage your walk with Christ?

And when the opportunity comes to share the gospel, are you willing to speak, even when it might make you uncomfortable?

I’m asking these questions because they’re things we should all be examining, myself included.

The armor of God isn’t just something we’re supposed to know about.

It’s something we’re commanded to put on.

And putting it on isn’t about becoming spiritually impressive or pretending we’re strong enough to handle whatever comes our way.

It’s about recognizing that we aren’t.

We need Christ.

We need His Word.

We need prayer.

We need one another.

And we need to be willing to stand firm in the truth, regardless of what the world around us thinks.

Because the battle is real.

But we were never commanded to fight it in our own strength.

So here’s the question.

When the enemy comes against you, when your faith is tested, and when following Christ requires courage rather than convenience, will you be standing firm?

Or will you realize that you’ve spent more time learning about the armor than actually putting it on?
