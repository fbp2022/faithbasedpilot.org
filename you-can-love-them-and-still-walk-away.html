You Can Love Them and Still Walk Away

October 9, 2026  •  9 min read

There’s something I’ve been thinking about when it comes to Christian dating, and I think it’s something we need to talk about more openly.

Missionary dating.

If you’re not familiar with the term, it’s when a Christian enters a romantic relationship with someone who isn’t a believer, hoping that through the relationship, they can eventually lead that person to Christ.

Sounds like a good intention, doesn’t it?

After all, as Christians, we’re called to share the gospel. We want people to know Jesus. We want to see lives transformed, hearts changed, and souls saved.

But here’s where I have a problem.

When did the Great Commission become permission to compromise our own walk with Christ for the possibility of changing someone else’s?

Now, before someone tells me about the couple they know who started dating when one wasn’t a Christian, and now they’re both serving the Lord together, I know.

Those stories exist.

And praise God for them! I genuinely mean that. When someone comes to know Christ, regardless of the circumstances that brought them to Him, that’s something worth celebrating.

But just because God brought something good out of a relationship doesn’t mean entering that relationship was necessarily a wise decision.

Those are two different things.

Paul warns believers in 2 Corinthians 6:14 about being unequally yoked with unbelievers. That passage isn’t specifically about modern dating, but the principle of spiritual unity matters tremendously when considering someone you may eventually marry.

And let’s be clear about something. I’m not saying we should avoid unbelievers, refuse to be friends with them, or stop sharing the gospel.

I’m saying there’s a difference between witnessing to someone and becoming romantically attached to someone in the hope that they’ll eventually believe what you believe.

Because sometimes, while you’re trying to bring them closer to Christ, you don’t realize how far you’ve allowed yourself to drift from Him.

And that brings me to what I consider the greater danger.

Imagine being a Christian who genuinely wants to grow closer to the Lord.

You want godly friendships. You want fellowship. You want to become more involved in church, strengthen your understanding of Scripture, and surround yourself with people who will encourage you in your faith.

But you’re dating someone who doesn’t share those priorities.

At first, maybe it doesn’t seem like a big deal. You miss a church gathering to spend time together. You turn down an invitation from some friends. You stop participating in something that was previously important to you because your partner doesn’t particularly like it.

Then it happens again.

And again.

Eventually, you’re barely seeing the people who used to encourage you spiritually. Your family notices you’ve become distant. Your friendships aren’t what they used to be, and those things you said were important to you somehow keep getting pushed aside.

Yet you’re still in the relationship.

You still love that person.

And you still believe things will eventually change.

Here’s my question.

If you entered this relationship believing you would lead someone closer to Christ, why are you the one becoming increasingly distant from the things that were helping you pursue Him?

That’s not something we should casually dismiss.

Hebrews 10:24–25 teaches believers to encourage one another toward love and good works and not neglect gathering together.

Christian fellowship isn’t just something we participate in when our schedules happen to allow it. We need other believers. We need encouragement, correction, accountability, and people who will tell us the truth even when it’s uncomfortable.

And this is where something much more serious can begin happening.

I’ve written before about manipulation and control in my paper Recognizing a Jezebelian Spirit. One of the dangers I discussed was isolation—separating someone from friends and family while undermining the relationships that might otherwise help them recognize what’s happening.

And I believe there’s a broader spiritual danger here.

Ephesians 6:11–12 reminds us that spiritual warfare is real and that Christians must remain alert to the schemes of the devil.

But we need to understand something about abuse.

It isn’t always physical.

Sometimes it’s emotional manipulation. Sometimes it’s intimidation, guilt, constant criticism, or making someone feel responsible for every problem in the relationship.

Sometimes it’s making a person feel guilty for spending time with their friends or family.

Sometimes it’s using Scripture to demand obedience while refusing to be held accountable to that same Scripture.

And sometimes it’s slowly convincing someone that everybody else is the problem until their romantic partner becomes the only person they feel they can rely upon.

Not every difficult relationship is abusive, and being an unbeliever doesn’t automatically make someone manipulative or dangerous. But repeated attempts to isolate, intimidate, or control another person are serious warning signs.

You don’t have to identify some particular demonic spirit to recognize harmful behavior.

And you certainly shouldn’t have to wait until someone physically hurts you before acknowledging that abuse is taking place.

Here’s what’s particularly troubling when missionary dating is involved.

A Christian may recognize these things are happening but continue making excuses because they believe they’re supposed to stay and lead their partner to Christ.

They begin confusing endurance with obedience.

But those aren’t always the same thing.

Now suppose the couple begins reading Scripture together every morning.

That’s wonderful, right?

Well, potentially.

I would never discourage someone from reading God’s Word. Scripture has the power to confront us with truth, expose sin, and teach us how we’re supposed to live.

But I want to challenge something.

Does reading the Bible together automatically mean the relationship is producing spiritual fruit?

No.

James 1:22 (LSB) says:

“But become doers of the word, and not merely hearers who delude themselves.”

Think about that.

You can read about love without demonstrating it. You can read about repentance without repenting. You can read about Christian fellowship while continuing to isolate yourself—or allowing someone else to isolate you—from godly relationships.

And you can talk about becoming more Christlike without making any meaningful effort to change.

Galatians 5:22–23 identifies the fruit of the Spirit, including love, joy, peace, patience, kindness, goodness, faithfulness, gentleness, and self-control.

Those are qualities that should increasingly become evident in how we live and treat others.

I’m not saying we should expect perfection. I certainly haven’t achieved it, and neither has anyone else walking this earth.

We all struggle.

But struggling with sin and becoming comfortable excusing it are two very different things.

If a couple begins reading Scripture but the manipulation continues, the isolation continues, and the same destructive behaviors remain, we need to stop assuming that the presence of a devotional somehow makes everything okay.

Bible reading is good.

But reading Scripture without allowing it to challenge how we live is missing the point.

I also want to address something about biblical leadership.

Men, if you desire to become husbands and fathers, you need to understand the responsibility that comes with that calling.

Ephesians 5:25 commands husbands to love their wives as Christ loved the church. That is sacrificial leadership. It’s not about controlling a woman or getting your own way.

It’s about loving, serving, protecting, and setting a godly example.

And if you’re pursuing marriage, you should be developing that character long before you stand at the altar.

You cannot claim to be preparing to lead a family toward Christ while continually setting aside your convictions merely to keep a romantic relationship alive.

Women also have a responsibility to pursue godliness and encourage spiritual growth. Proverbs 31 describes a woman characterized by wisdom, strength, diligence, and reverence for Yahweh.

A godly woman shouldn’t have to manufacture a man’s relationship with Christ for him, just as a godly man shouldn’t have to force a woman to pursue the Lord.

Both individuals must personally desire to follow Christ.

And remember, dating isn’t marriage. A boyfriend doesn’t acquire the biblical role of a husband simply because the relationship might one day lead to marriage.

Biblical leadership is not about making someone follow you. It’s about faithfully following Christ and demonstrating His character.

If children are involved, our responsibility to model godliness, provide stability, and protect them becomes even more important.

This may be the hardest part for someone who entered missionary dating with sincere intentions.

You’ve invested time, emotion, energy, and prayer into someone.

You’ve seen moments when they seemed interested in Christ. Maybe they even started reading the Bible with you.

You remember those moments.

You hold onto them.

And you begin convincing yourself that if you just stay a little longer, things might finally change.

But what happens when they don’t?

What happens when the relationship continues damaging your spiritual life, distancing you from others, or exposing you to abuse?

Are you supposed to stay indefinitely because you’re afraid leaving will ruin their opportunity to know Jesus?

No.

Paul says in 1 Corinthians 3:6 (LSB):

“I planted, Apollos watered, but God was causing the growth.”

Paul understood something we sometimes forget.

We are not the ones who save people. God is.

We can share the gospel. We can pray. We can encourage. We can demonstrate Christ’s love.

But we cannot force genuine repentance, manufacture faith, or change another person’s heart.

That responsibility was never ours.

And that means we need to stop treating romantic commitment as though it were necessary for someone’s salvation.

You can genuinely love someone and recognize that remaining in the relationship is no longer wise.

You can forgive someone without returning to the same destructive circumstances.

You can pray for someone while establishing boundaries that protect you.

And when an unmarried relationship becomes abusive, you are not required to remain in it to prove your faithfulness to Christ.

If you’re being threatened or controlled, don’t assume you must confront the person alone. Seek trustworthy help and make a safe plan, particularly when children are involved.

Walking away from abuse isn’t abandoning the gospel.

I want to leave you with one question.

If you’re in a relationship that began with the hope of bringing someone closer to Christ, take an honest look at where you are today.

Not where you hope to be five years from now.

Not who you imagine your partner might eventually become.

Where are you today?

Are you growing closer to the Lord? Are you building godly friendships, participating in Christian fellowship, and becoming more obedient to Scripture?

Is your relationship encouraging those things?

Or have you slowly surrendered them because keeping the relationship together has become more important than everything else?

I’m not suggesting every difficult season means a relationship should end. Relationships require patience, forgiveness, humility, and work.

But patience isn’t permission for abuse. Forgiveness isn’t the removal of accountability. And love isn’t a requirement to stay in a relationship that is systematically harming you.

The Great Commission is a command to make disciples.

It is not a command to date someone into salvation.

So share the gospel. Love others. Pray for those who don’t know Christ.

But don’t allow your desire to see someone saved to become the reason you ignore what is happening to your own walk with the Lord.

You are not their savior.

Jesus Christ is.

And you can love someone, desire their salvation, and still recognize when it’s time to walk away.
