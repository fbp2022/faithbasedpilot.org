When Your Heart Aches for the Lord

November 21, 2026  •  9 min read

There are moments in worship when words simply aren’t enough. You may be listening to a familiar song, reflecting on the words, and thinking about the goodness of God when suddenly your heart begins to ache. There’s a longing to be closer to the Lord, a desire to know Him more deeply, and sometimes, without expecting it, tears begin to fall.

Not necessarily because you’re sad or going through something difficult. Sometimes your heart is simply overwhelmed by the love you have for the Lord and the realization of how much He has loved you.

It’s an interesting thing to consider. How can we long so deeply for someone we have never seen with our own eyes? How can the thought of Jesus Christ, His sacrifice, His mercy, and the promise of one day being in His presence bring us to tears?

I believe Scripture gives us a beautiful understanding of this longing.

In Psalm 42:1–2, the psalmist compares his desire for God to a deer panting for streams of water. His soul thirsts for the living God, and he asks when he will come and appear before Him. Think about what he’s saying. He isn’t merely asking God to provide something for him. His soul is longing for God Himself.

There’s a difference between wanting what God can give us and wanting God because of who He is.

How often do we approach the Lord because we need something? Perhaps we need wisdom, protection, forgiveness, comfort, or an answer to prayer. There’s nothing wrong with bringing our needs before Him. Scripture encourages us to do so. But how often do we come before Him simply because we love Him? Not asking for anything, not expecting anything in return, but genuinely wanting to spend time with Him.

David expresses something similar in Psalm 63:1 when he speaks of earnestly seeking God, describing his soul as thirsty and his flesh as yearning for Him in a dry and weary land where there is no water. That is a powerful description of a heart that recognizes its need for the living God.

And perhaps that’s part of what happens during those moments of worship when tears begin to fall. The heart recognizes something that is difficult to explain with words. It longs for the Lord.

We tend to associate crying with sadness, pain, or weakness. Yet why should we be ashamed of being moved to tears while worshiping our Creator? Jesus Himself wept, as John 11:35 tells us. Tears are not inherently a sign of weakness, nor are they always an expression of sorrow.

Consider what Christ has done for us. The eternal Son of God, who was without sin, willingly humbled Himself and became obedient to death, even death on a cross, as Paul explains in Philippians 2:5–8. He suffered for sins He did not commit so that sinners who could never make themselves righteous before God might be forgiven and reconciled to Him.

We speak of these truths often. We read about the crucifixion, sing about the resurrection, and thank the Lord for His grace. But sometimes I wonder whether we have become so familiar with these things that we fail to stop and consider their tremendous weight.

The Creator of all things, the One through whom everything was made, willingly suffered for those who had rebelled against Him. We did nothing to deserve that mercy. We could never earn it. Yet God demonstrated His love toward us in that while we were still sinners, Christ died for us (Romans 5:8).

How can a heart that truly understands even a portion of that love not be moved by it?

Of course, tears alone do not demonstrate genuine worship. We must be careful not to confuse an emotional experience with spiritual maturity. Someone can become deeply emotional during a song without truly considering the Lord, just as another believer may worship with complete sincerity without shedding a single tear. Jesus teaches in John 4:23–24 that true worshipers worship the Father in spirit and truth. Our worship must be grounded in the truth of God’s Word, not merely in what we feel.

But I also don’t believe we should be so afraid of emotion that we suppress the genuine affection and gratitude we have for our Savior. If reflecting on His holiness, mercy, and sacrifice brings a believer to tears, why should that be considered something embarrassing?

There is something remarkable about the Christian’s relationship with Jesus Christ. We have never seen His face. We have not physically walked beside Him or heard His voice as the disciples did. Yet through His Word we know Him, and through faith we love Him.

Peter speaks of this in 1 Peter 1:8–9, describing believers who love Christ despite not having seen Him, who believe in Him, and who rejoice with a joy that cannot adequately be expressed.

Think about that. Loving someone you have never physically seen, trusting Him with your life, and longing for the day when you will finally stand before Him.

Paul understood this longing. In Philippians 1:23, he expresses his desire to depart and be with Christ, while also acknowledging the importance of remaining to serve others. Paul wasn’t speaking as someone who had given up on life. He understood that being with Christ was far better, yet he also understood that the Lord still had work for him to do.

That is something I believe we should consider more often. As Christians, we look forward to eternity, but what exactly are we looking forward to? Is it simply the absence of suffering, sickness, death, and the burdens of this world? Certainly those promises are wonderful, but they are not the greatest blessing awaiting us.

The greatest blessing is Christ Himself.

Revelation 22:4 tells us that His servants will see His face. One day, those who belong to Christ will no longer walk by faith in the same way they do now. They will be in His presence.

Perhaps that helps explain why the heart can ache for the Lord even while we know He is with us. We have His Word, His promises, and the indwelling Holy Spirit, yet we still look forward to the day when we will see our Savior face to face.

There is a longing that comes with loving someone and looking forward to being with them. And when that love is directed toward the Lord, the longing can be difficult to put into words.

Yet there is something else we must remember. Worship cannot end when the music stops.

It is possible to be deeply moved while singing about the Lord and then go about our lives without allowing the truths we have sung to change the way we live. We can sing about surrender while refusing to surrender something in our own lives. We can sing about forgiveness while holding bitterness toward someone else. We can sing about loving Christ while neglecting His Word and disregarding His commandments.

Jesus says in John 14:15, “If you love Me, you will keep My commandments.”

That should cause us to examine ourselves.

If we truly love the Lord, that love should extend beyond the emotions we experience during worship. It should be evident in our obedience, our repentance, our treatment of others, and our willingness to follow Him even when doing so is difficult.

Romans 12:1 calls believers to present their bodies as a living and holy sacrifice to God, describing this as their spiritual service of worship. Worship isn’t merely something we do during a church service or while listening to music. Our lives should be an expression of worship to the Lord.

And that brings us back to the longing of the heart.

Psalm 84:2 describes a soul that longs and even yearns for the courts of the Lord, with the heart and flesh crying out to the living God. The psalmist wasn’t simply expressing appreciation for a place or a religious tradition. He was expressing a deep desire for fellowship with God.

I wonder how often we genuinely feel that kind of longing. Not whether we become emotional or cry during a song, but whether our hearts truly desire the Lord above the things of this world.

Do we seek Him because we love Him, or primarily because we want something from Him? Do we spend time in His Word because we desire to know Him, or merely because we feel obligated to? When we pray, do we take time to praise Him and thank Him for who He is, or do we move immediately to the things we need?

These questions aren’t meant to suggest that our love for the Lord will always produce some extraordinary feeling. There will be days when worship feels difficult, when our minds wander, and when the burdens of life weigh heavily upon us. God does not become any less worthy of worship during those moments.

Our faith cannot depend on whether we feel particularly close to Him on any given day. The truth of His Word remains unchanged, and His promises do not become less certain when our emotions are quiet.

But there is nothing wrong with having a heart that aches for the Lord. There is nothing shameful about tears that come from reflecting on His goodness, His holiness, and the sacrifice of His Son.

We shouldn’t seek the tears themselves. We should seek Christ.

And as we grow in our understanding of who He is, may our love for Him deepen. May we never become so familiar with the message of the cross that we cease to be humbled by it. May we never become so occupied with the temporary things of this world that we forget the eternal hope we have in Him.

One day, faith will give way to sight. The Lord whom we have loved without seeing will be before us, and the longing that has accompanied our earthly walk will find its fulfillment in His presence.

Until then, may we continue seeking Him through His Word, approaching Him in prayer, worshiping Him in spirit and truth, and living in obedience to His commandments.

And whether our worship is accompanied by tears, joyful singing, or quiet reverence, may our hearts remain fixed upon the One who is worthy of it all.

Not because of what He can give us, but because of who He is.

God is good all the time; all the time God is good.
